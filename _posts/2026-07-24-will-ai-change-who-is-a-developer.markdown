In 1966, J Paul Getty became the world's richest man. But he couldn't
put his finger on what made him so successful.[^1]

> The difference between a successful businessman and one possibly not so successful is that maybe 37 different qualities are required for a great success, and [if] a man has 35 of those qualities, he makes a more modest success. But just what those two missing qualities might be, I don't know.

I suspect there's something to that. There isn't one thing that leads us
to success. And it's not just a matter of how successful you are. A set
of unknown qualities guides our life paths. We all benefit from the
luck, traits, and skills that make us who we are.

That idea got me thinking about the changes the software development field is going
through. I suspect there are a set of qualities that most developers share. Of
course every developer is unique. While we may share a set of overlapping
qualities, we are not just developers. We are people. So imagine that most
developers have twenty common properties. You and I might have those twenty
in common, but still have a hundred that differ. Of our twenty common qualities,
we likely share more than enablers for success. We also share qualities that
make us want to be developers and make it possible for us to become developers. For
example, before becoming developers we may share curiosity and computer access.

AI is dramatically changing the nature of our work. We hear a lot about the
need to adapt, but what will happen longer term? If the nature of the work
changes, then it's possible that the set of common properties will change too.
Imagine that only eighteen of our common properties remain relevant and two
new common properties are introduced. If we look at the pool of non-developers,
this shift changes the subset of the population that will choose to be
developers in the future. Remember that this goes beyond who is capable of
being a developer. This is also considering who wants to be a developer.

We would not see the impact immediately because most existing developers
entered the field based on the old property set. However, as new people
join the field and others retire, we could see a signicant generational change.
Over time, the industry changes signicantly not just because the technology changed,
but because the humans who make up the industry changed. For example,
imagine if the new set of qualities is highly correlated with loving
cargo pants. Cargo pants are irrelevant to being a developer, but
you still see a population change where a strong majority of people wear
cargo pants every day. Who knows? One day cargo pants could become
mandatory in the office. While this is a silly example, it illustrates
the possibility of a shift that is unpredictable. It's insufficient to
make predictions about AI. You also need to know all of the correlated
qualities.

Like J Paul Getty, I don't think we know what the underlying qualities
for developers are in the past, present, or the future. We may have inklings
of several, but we don't know the complete set. And we certainly don't
know all correlated, but unnecessary traits that come along for the ride.
Over time, maybe we will find AI has created a largely changed work
place not directly because of AI capabilities, but because the people
we eat lunch with each day will have changed.
